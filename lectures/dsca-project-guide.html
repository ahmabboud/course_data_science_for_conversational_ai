The team project: a guide

Start here

Choose a paper, reproduce it, add your own idea, defend it

Definitions

Baseline: the starting point you reproduce. Extension: your own improvement or test on top of it. Defense: the session (Module 6) where each team presents and answers questions.

What you actually produce

First reproduce the paper's number, then try to improve on it

An example from the paper list

For the Gorilla paper, a team could run the authors' released model on part of the test set (the baseline, number A). Then it could try an idea the paper leaves open, such as a new kind of API that the test set does not cover (the extension, number B). The comparison of B with A is the heart of the project. Smoke test: a small run that stands in for a full run. It is allowed if you can explain what a full run would need.

Naming a paper is not an approved pitch

The pitch step still applies to every team, whichever paper you choose. A paper with working code only means less of your time goes to setting up the baseline, so more goes to the part that is yours.

What is allowed, and the cost limit

Five rules for choosing your paper

  1. Team size: two or three people.
  2. One topic and one paper for the whole project.
  3. Cost limit: it must run on a personal computer, or cost at most $20 in API fees in total.
  4. A smoke test is fine instead of a full run, if you can explain what a full run would need.
  5. Any paper on the menu, or your own, with the instructor's approval of your pitch.
Two things that are not a problem

Another team chooses the same paper. You choose a paper that was taught in a module. Neither is restricted or penalized. What counts is the extension you add.

Why the pitch and the report ask for honesty

Two checks that keep the project honest

Before the work: the pitch approval

The instructor reads your pitch to catch one thing: an idea that already exists as someone else's published follow-up paper. Finding that out after weeks of work is the worst time.

After the work: related-work honesty

Your report must list related follow-up work on the same paper and say how your extension is different. Saying nothing about it counts against you. If you found none, say so.

Each rule solves a different problem

Three team rules, and the problem each one solves

RuleWhat it meansThe problem it solves
1. One branch and one pull request per personWork on your own branch (for example alice/baseline-eval-loop). When ready, open a pull request into main yourself. Never commit straight to main.Two people editing the same file at the same time.
2. Every pull request needs a teammate's approvalAnother teammate reads the changes, runs them and asks questions before merging. A quick "looks good" is not a review.Knowing who wrote what, for learning and for grading.
3. Always commit under your own GitHub accountNever share a login and never commit for someone else. If you worked as a pair, both of you push from your own branch.Making rules 1 and 2 mean something.
Definitions

Branch: your own copy of the work inside the repository. Pull request: a request to merge your branch into main, which a teammate can review.

Four checkpoints in your repository, in order

Four GitHub issues, four checkpoints

IssueIt is done when...
1. Idea pitchThe instructor has approved it (a checkbox in the issue).
2. Baseline reproductionThe baseline runs end to end and the commands are in baseline/README.md. At least one of the paper's numbers is reproduced, with your number beside the paper's. A smoke test is explained. The cost limit is respected.
3. ExtensionThe extension runs end to end and is compared directly with the baseline. Results are credible, or a smoke test is stated plainly. The numbers are ready for the report. The cost limit is respected.
4. Report and slidesThe report is complete, related work is cited, and any smoke test is stated. The slides follow the required structure. Both are handed in before Module 6.

For each issue: assign it to the teammates who will work on it, write who does which part in its checklist, and link your pull requests to it (Closes #number).

A short paper about your own work

The report has five sections

SectionWhat goes there
Abstract3 to 5 sentences: what the paper does, what limitation you targeted, what you built, the main result. Write it last.
1. The baseline paperIts problem, its method (the real mechanism, not only its name) and its results with numbers.
2. Your extensionWhy (the limitation), what you built (enough to reproduce it) and notes on running your code.
3. ResultsYour reproduction next to the paper's numbers, your extension against the baseline, and an honest assessment of what worked and what did not.
4. Related workPublished follow-up work on the same paper, and how your extension differs. If none, say so.
5. Limits and full validationIf you used a smoke test, exactly what a full run would need: data, compute, time or cost.
A hard limit, not a suggestion

At most 8 slides: a cover, up to 6 content slides, and references

  • Cover: team name, paper, topic. Content (no more than 6): the baseline paper's problem, solution and results, then your extension's idea and results, in that order.
  • References: full citations for the paper and any related work in your report.
  • Numbers from your comparison belong on the slides. Long method text does not.

Module 6

The defense: 12 minutes to present, 12 minutes of questions

Good to know

Module 6 is 180 minutes, and the instructor may extend it by up to one hour if there are many teams. No team's time is shortened to fit. If you plan a live demo, check that it already works before your slot.

85 of the 100 points

Part A: what each line of the project rubric asks

CriterionScoreThe question the graders askFull marks look like
Code and reproduction quality25Does it run, and is it correct?The paper's method runs on your computer, and your own extension is really built and works.
Understanding of the research25Do you understand the paper?You explain the paper's problem, method and results, and answer questions about all of it, not only your own part.
Motivation and value25Why is your idea worth doing?It fixes a real, specific weakness of the paper and is useful, not new only for the sake of being new.
Result quality25Did it actually work?A real comparison with the baseline and believable results. A small test run is fine if you say what a full run would need.

Each line is scored out of 25 (100 in all). The total is multiplied by 0.85, which gives the 85 points of Part A. The defense score belongs to the team.

15 of the 100 points

Part B: 2.5 points for each of the 6 sessions

WhatPointsHow it is scored
Attendance1.5 a sessionYou are there and you stay for the session.
Participation1.0 a session1 = you asked a question, answered one, or joined the discussion at least once. 0.5 = you joined only when asked directly. 0 = no contribution.
One excused absenceNo lossYou may miss one module. It keeps its 2.5 points if you finish the missed project work before the next module. Any other absence loses that session's points.

Labs are demonstrations run by the instructor, so participation means taking part in the demonstration and the discussion. Final grade = 0.85 x defense score + Part B points. Example: 88 at the defense and 13 for Part B gives 74.8 + 13 = 87.8. Part B is yours alone, not the team's.

Prepare every teammate for every part

Any teammate can be asked about any part

About...Examples of questions (invented to show the style)
The paperWhat problem does the paper solve? How does its method work, step by step? What do its results show, and what do they not show?
Your extensionWhich weakness of the paper does it target? Why is that worth fixing? What did you change, and why that?
The codeShow me where in the code this step happens. What happens if this input is empty? How did you get this number?
Your resultsHow did you compare with the baseline? Was it a full run or a smoke test? What would change at full size?
How to prepare

Do not split the project into "my part" and "your part". Each of you should be able to explain the paper, the extension and the code. Practice by asking each other these questions, and read each other's pull requests properly.

Checklist: are you ready to defend?

  • The pitch is approved, and the report says if the idea changed.
  • The baseline runs, with the commands in baseline/README.md.
  • Your number is next to the paper's number.
  • The extension runs and is compared with the baseline.
  • Any smoke test is stated, with what a full run would need.
  • The report is complete and cites related work.
  • The slides: a cover, at most 6 content slides, references.
  • The cost stayed under $20, or it ran on a personal computer.
  • Everything is handed in before Module 6.
  • Each teammate can answer about the paper, the extension and the code.
